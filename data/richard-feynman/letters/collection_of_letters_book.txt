About the book
In *Perfectly Reasonable Deviations From the Beaten Track*,
readers are invited into the rich and multifaceted world of
Richard P. Feynman—a Nobel Prize-winning physicist,
devoted family man, passionate educator, talented bongo
player, and a true intellectual powerhouse. This collection of
over forty years of Feynman's letters offers an unparalleled
glimpse into the mind of a man whose vibrant spirit and
insatiable curiosity made him a legendary figure of his time.
Featuring correspondence with esteemed scientists like Victor
Weisskopf, Stephen Wolfram, James Watson, and Edward
Teller, alongside heartfelt exchanges with fans, students, and
family, this anthology sheds light on the personal connections
that significantly shaped modern science. Edited by his
daughter Michelle, the book reveals the wisdom and fervor of
a personality that resonated deeply with many yet remained
profoundly private. *Perfectly Reasonable Deviations From
the Beaten Track* stands as a compelling testament to
Feynman's legacy and an essential read for fans and
newcomers alike, offering insights into one of the twentieth
century’s most iconic thinkers.
About the author
Richard P. Feynman was a renowned American physicist
celebrated for his groundbreaking contributions to quantum
mechanics, particularly the path integral formulation and
quantum electrodynamics, which earned him the Nobel Prize
in Physics in 1965 alongside Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro
Tomonaga. He is also known for his work on superfluidity in
supercooled liquid helium and his innovative parton model in
particle physics. Feynman's development of the visually
intuitive Feynman diagrams transformed the representation of
subatomic particle interactions. Beyond his theoretical
achievements, he played a key role in the Manhattan Project
and later investigated the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster. A
pioneer in quantum computing and nanotechnology, Feynman
held the Richard Chace Tolman professorship at Caltech and
remains one of the most recognizable scientists of his time.

Chapter 1 Summary: Early Years and Personal
Letters of Richard Feynman
Background
Richard Feynman, after completing his undergraduate studies
at MIT in June 1939, pursued graduate studies at Princeton
University, heeding Professor Slater’s advice to explore the
wider world. During this period, he shared his experiences
through letters to his family, revealing his teaching
experiences, student life challenges, and a romantic
relationship with Arline Greenbaum, whom he married
shortly after earning his Ph.D. in June 1942.
Themes in Feynman's Letters
Feynman’s letters from these years reflect youthful
exuberance and an emerging intellect, marked by a capacity
for keen observations and decision-making. He shows a
playful yet serious approach to life, often commenting on
time and personal routine with casual ambivalence toward
formal details.
Early Experiences and Challenges
In his letters, Feynman expressed eagerness regarding
teaching mechanics, his financial struggles with college
expenses, and shared plans for family visits. He also faced
academic challenges, notably grappling with complex
mathematical problems, which fueled his enthusiasm for
physics. His passion was reflected in his early contributions
to research, including a co-authored paper on cosmic rays.
Personal Correspondences
Throughout his correspondence with family, Feynman
maintained a light-hearted yet sincere tone, discussing
regular activities and social life, and sharing both trivial and
significant events. His exchanges with Arline reveal a deep
affection and a desire to support each other in both personal
and professional realms.
Marriage and Family Concerns
Feynman’s letters to his mother expressed tension due to her
concerns regarding his marriage to Arline, particularly about
her health (she had tuberculosis) and the potential impacts on
his career and finances. Despite these concerns, Feynman
asserted his commitment to marriage, emphasizing his love
and willingness to take responsibility.
War Efforts and Career Developments
As the war approached, Feynman received job offers to
contribute to military projects, illustrating his growing stature
in the scientific community and his involvement in the war
effort. Throughout this time, he continued to balance
personal life and rising academic responsibilities,
communicating his experiences in a blend of humor and
seriousness.
In summary, the early letters of Richard Feynman provide a
vivid glimpse into his life as a young physicist, student,
husband, and budding professional amidst the backdrop of
World War II, characterized by insightful reflections, keen
observations, and heartfelt family connections.

Chapter 4 Summary : 1960–1970: The
National Academy of Sciences
Chapter 4 Summary: The National Academy of
Sciences
Background and Initial Correspondence
In November 1959, Richard P. Feynman received a letter
regarding unpaid dues for his National Academy of Sciences
membership. This correspondence sparked a series of
exchanges about the meaning of honors and the meritocratic
nature of exclusive societies.
Feynman Resigns
Feynman expressed a lack of interest in the Academy, stating
he would resign. By February 1961, he officially
communicated his decision to resign due to a personal
disinterest in the Academy's activities.
A Conversation about Membership
The President of the Academy, Detlev W. Bronk, responded
to Feynman's resignation urging him to reconsider his
decision. He acknowledged the triviality of dues and admired
Feynman's contributions to science. Feynman reiterated his
feelings about membership and the psychological discomfort
he experienced with judging merit, describing membership in
the Academy as self-agrandising.
Further Resignations and Responses
Over the years, Feynman continued to submit resignations
for various personal reasons, always noting that his reasons
were not criticisms of the Academy itself. His
correspondences highlighted the personal nature of his
decisions and a desire for privacy regarding the matter.
Conclusion
Ultimately, Feynman's resignations from the National
Academy of Sciences stemmed from personal psychological
quirks rather than dissent or dissatisfaction with the
organization. Despite the Academy's efforts to retain him and
adjustments to its structure, Feynman remained resolute in
his decision.
Critical Thinking
Key Point:Feynman's Critique of Meritocracy in
Exclusive Societies
Critical Interpretation:Feynman's resignation and
reflections on the National Academy of Sciences
illuminate his skepticism toward meritocratic structures,
revealing a profound discomfort with the notion of
valuing individuals based on accomplishments in a rigid
hierarchy. While he respected scientific excellence, his
stance invites contemplation on the societal perception
of merit and prestige that often engenders elitism,
questioning whether such systems genuinely reflect
contributions to knowledge or merely reinforce
exclusive identities. Feynman's perspective can be
supported by scholars like Pierre Bourdieu, who
critiques social structures and the arbitrary measures of
success in elite institutions.
Chapter 5 Summary : 1960–1965
Summary of Chapter 5: Richard P. Feynman
Introduction to Molecular Biology (1960-1965)
Feynman engaged in molecular biology research at Caltech,
collaborating with Bob Edgar on bacterial viruses, leading to
the discovery of back mutations that potentially shed light on
DNA sequencing. He was encouraged by Edgar to publish
his findings, resulting in a notable talk at Harvard.
Personal Life and Early Fame
Feynman's marriage to Gweneth occurred in September
1960, marking a shift in his focus towards domestic life as
well as a growing reputation in popular science. His role as a
scientific advisor for the television program “About Time”
helped introduce him to a wider audience.
Awards and Publications
In 1962, he received the E. O. Lawrence Award from the
Department of Energy; the same period saw the creation of
"The Feynman Lectures on Physics," co-edited with Robert
B. Leighton and Matthew Sands, which became a seminal
educational resource. This period also included the famous
Lectures on Gravitation and discussions of his instructional
sessions captured in film.
Nanotechnology Vision
Feynman's 1959 lecture, “There Is Plenty of Room at the
Bottom,” laid the groundwork for nanotechnology by
proposing the miniaturization of information storage and
devices, offering prizes for practical demonstrations.
Correspondence with Students and Colleagues
Throughout the chapter, Feynman corresponded with various
individuals, including students and fellow scientists,
discussing topics from educational pathways in physics to the
significance of mathematical understanding in physics.
Reflections on Science and Ethics
Feynman engaged in discussions regarding the intersection of
science and ethics. He articulated the belief that moral values
should not be derived solely from scientific reasoning but
acknowledged the role science plays in understanding the
implications of ethical choices.
Lectures and Public Engagement
His involvement in various talks, including the MIT
Centennial and the research for his Messenger Lectures,
showcased his commitment to science education and
communication, striving to make complex scientific
principles accessible to the general public.
Concluding Thoughts
Feynman's letters revealed his candid personality, inviting a
sense of warmth and humor into scientific discourse, while
also reflecting his dedication to education and ethical
considerations in the scientific community.

Chapter 7 Summary : 1966–1969
Chapter 7 Summary
Nobel Prize Recognition and Curriculum Work
Between 1966 and 1969, Richard Feynman's Nobel Prize in
Physics significantly increased his international recognition,
leading him to decline numerous requests for his time,
although he continued contributing to the California State
Curriculum Committee despite having resigned in 1964. He
focused on improving science education, critiquing science
textbooks, and emphasizing the importance of understanding
over rote memorization.
Correspondence and Impact
Feynman's letters highlight his interactions with various
admirers and educators, including Dr. Virendra K. Singh
from India, who expressed gratitude for Feynman's
approachable writing style. Through his responses, Feynman
underscored the importance of clear communication in
teaching complex scientific concepts.
Honorary Degrees and Personal Milestones
In 1967, Feynman declined his first honorary degree. During
this period, he also adopted a son, which marked a significant
personal milestone in his life.
Teaching Philosophy
Feynman engaged with inquiries about physics education
from students and colleagues, emphasizing learning through
exploration and critical thinking rather than memorization.
His businesslike yet insightful approach to teaching remained
evident. He encouraged students to engage in physics
discussions and explore concepts until they would be able to
explain them independently.
Critiques of Popular Science Communication
Feynman was reflective about public understanding of
science, sharing thoughts on modern poetry and the
intersection with scientific phenomena. He voiced concern
about how contemporary poets failed to express the beauty of
scientific understanding and nature.
Reflections on Scholarship and Science
Acknowledging the struggles of aspiring scientists and the
potential challenges of learning physics, Feynman provided
guidance and motivation, advocating that students find joy in
their learning process. He also expressed skepticism
regarding the effectiveness of certain educational approaches
and remained committed to promoting genuine curiosity and
understanding in science.
Final Thoughts
As Feynman navigated through accolades, personal
developments, and the complexities of teaching, his
correspondence depicted a profound commitment to science
education, a firm stance against accepting honorary
recognition, and a continued pursuit of knowledge and
sharing that knowledge effectively with others. His unique
blend of humility and brilliance permeated the narratives
collected in this chapter.
Chapter 8 Summary : 1970–1975
Chapter 8 Summary
Achievements (1970-1975)

Richard P. Feynman received several prestigious awards
during this period, including the Oersted Medal in 1972 and
the Niels Bohr International Gold Medal in 1973, which
recognized his contributions to physics and education. His
research included the development of the concept of partons,
still relevant today, and he authored specialized textbooks on
statistical mechanics and particle interactions.
Interest in Political Issues

Feynman engaged with political matters of the time,
responding to letters that sought his opinion on scientific and
military ethics, particularly regarding his work on the atomic
bomb. He firmly stated his stance against visiting countries
with political exclusions.
Scientific Communication

Feynman encouraged communication between scientists and
the public. He received letters from various individuals and
offered insights on scientific topics, from educational
methods to the nature of physics. He advised students to
explore subjects independently, emphasizing the importance
of self-directed learning.
Response to Social Issues

Feynman addressed sensitive topics such as gender
discrimination in science, acknowledging the implications of
his words and the importance of equality in education. He
corresponded with individuals who expressed concerns about
the portrayal of women in scientific contexts.
Engagement with the Community

Feynman maintained correspondence with young scientists
and students, providing guidance and encouragement in their
educational endeavors. He stressed the significance of
nourishing gift ed minds within the public school system.
Philosophy on Science and Life

Throughout his responses, Feynman demonstrated a unique
blend of scientific rigor, humility, and personality. He
offered personal reflections on the responsibilities tied to
scientific advancements and their ethical implications. He
maintained a belief in the unpredictability of future
developments in nuclear energy and technology.
Personal Reflections

Feynman shared light-hearted moments with fans and
students, illustrating his approachable nature and the joy he
found in engaging with curious minds. He encouraged
creative exploration in science, stressing that great ideas
often stem from individual thought rather than collaborative
projects.
Final Thoughts

As a leading figure in physics, Feynman's work during this
era highlighted the importance of merging science with
human values and intellectual responsibility, motivating a
generation of students and scientists to explore the depths of
both their fields and themselves.

During the early 1980s, Richard Feynman starred in a
successful documentary titled "The Pleasure of Finding
Things Out." The program garnered significant attention,
leading to an influx of letters from viewers, showcasing
Feynman's ability to communicate complex ideas to a
broader audience. Over time, these exchanges revealed

Personal Struggles and Resilience
Feynman continued teaching and engaging with students
throughout his health challenges. Despite a serious fall
leading to a subdural hematoma and subsequent surgery, he
retained a sense of humor, joking about having "holes in his
head." His experiences prompted reflections on life's
fragility, especially after the loss of his mother.
Exchanges on Science and Life
Feynman engaged in various correspondences with admirers,
including discussions on transcendental meditation and its
role in mental clarity. He expressed skepticism toward its
effectiveness compared to rigorous scientific thought, as seen
in his discussions with Connie Griffiths and J. Gerard Wolff
about language acquisition and computer simulations.
Reflections on Science and Humanity
Feynman's letters highlight his views on the relationship
between knowledge and responsibility, particularly in the
sciences. He corresponded with students and educators,
discussing the moral implications of scientific pursuits and
the importance of recognizing broader societal concerns.
Recognition and Comradery
His communication with peers, such as Dr. Blas Cabrera,
illustrated the collaborative nature of scientific inquiry.
Feynman defended Cabrera’s interpretations of his monopole
experiment against misconceptions, reinforcing the value of
clear scientific communication.
Connections to Personal Experiences
Amidst his professional responsibilities, Feynman shared
personal insights, revealing his values and perspectives on
wealth and morality through anecdotes from social
encounters. He expressed disdain for superficial markers of
success, emphasizing a deeper connection with intellectual
curiosity over accolades.
Encouragement to the Next Generation
Feynman maintained a nurturing relationship with his
children, encouraging their pursuits in diverse fields, while
also inspiring other young scientists through responsive,
thoughtful letters. He offered guidance to students like Eric
Leuliette and Frances Best, emphasizing the joy of discovery
in science and persistence in education.
Contribution to Science Communication
Throughout his correspondence, Feynman addressed the
divide between science and the humanities, cautioning
against divisive categorizations and encouraging
interdisciplinary exploration. His authentic engagement
extended beyond academic matters, focusing on the human
experience inherent in scientific discovery.

Criticism of ‘New Mathematics’

He critiques the term “new mathematics,” highlighting that
many concepts are outdated and have little relation to
practical use. The true innovation should be in teaching
approaches rather than content.
Teaching Methods

Feynman calls for a flexible approach to arithmetic that
encourages problem-solving through creativity and personal
exploration, rather than rigid methodologies. He stresses the
importance of nurturing an innovative mindset in students for
their future endeavors in technical fields.

traditional methods that constrain students to memorized
facts and limited techniques. He proposes that students
should explore multiple ways to find solutions, fostering
creativity and critical thinking.

encourages students to explore solutions through trial and
error. This flexibility prepares them for more complex
mathematical challenges they will encounter later.

Introduction to the Quark Search

The relentless pursuit of the quark—a fundamental particle
theorized to be the building blocks of matter—has been a
focal point for physicists globally. Despite innovative
methods employed in various environments, the quark
remains elusive, mirroring the mythical Snark. The quest for
the quark symbolizes a deeper understanding of matter rather
than practical applications.
Key Figures: Murray Gell-Mann and Richard
Feynman

Prominent physicists Murray Gell-Mann and Richard
Feynman are instrumental in this pursuit, both of whom have
received high accolades, including the Nobel Prize. They
blend showmanship with intellectual prowess, attracting
students and driving interest in theoretical physics.
The Origin of the Quark Concept

The term "quark" emerged during discussions between
Gell-Mann and Feynman, reflecting their innovative spirit.
Gell-Mann’s idea of naming it after a phrase from James
Joyce’s "Finnegans Wake" illustrates their whimsical
approach to serious scientific discourse.
Distinct Teaching Styles

Feynman, known for his dynamic and engaging lecturing
style, often employs theatrical elements and personal
anecdotes, while Gell-Mann favors a more intimate seminar
approach, emphasizing clarity and erudition. Despite their
differences, they share a passion for making complex physics
accessible.
Historical Context of Particle Physics

The chapter outlines the evolution of particle physics, tracing
the journey from early concepts of the atom to the
complexities of subatomic particles like neutrons and
protons. The discovery of the strong interaction and
subsequent theories, including Yukawa's meson, shaped the
understanding of matter.
Breakthroughs in Understanding Particle
Interactions

and the weak interaction, leading to significant advances in
physics, including the recognition that weak interactions
could violate conservation laws. This revelation transformed
collective understanding within the field.

theoretical expectations that underscored the beauty of
nature.
Philosophical and Aesthetic Reflections

nature, viewing the quest for understanding the structure of
matter as not only a scientific endeavor but a deeply human
pursuit echoing a longstanding philosophical question about
the fundamental nature of reality.

quest for knowledge about the universe's underlying
principles.

Chapter 13 | Appendix II: “Gravity, The
Disrespectful,” by Earl Ubell, Science Editor| Q&A
1.Question
What fundamental idea underlies Einstein's theory of
relativity?
Answer:Einstein's theory of relativity is built on the
concept that acceleration and gravity are
indistinguishable from one another, illustrated
through a thought experiment of a man in an
elevator. He cannot tell whether the force he feels is
due to the acceleration of the elevator or to the
gravitational pull from a massive body.
2.Question
How does the theory of general relativity change our
understanding of gravity?
Answer:General relativity proposes that gravity is not a force
in the traditional sense but rather a curvature of space-time
caused by mass. Instead of objects being drawn together by a
gravitational force, they move along curved paths in a
geometric fabric of the universe.
3.Question
What challenges do physicists face when trying to unify
gravity with quantum mechanics?
Answer:Unifying gravity with quantum mechanics involves
reconciling two fundamentally different theories: general
relativity, which describes gravity on a large scale, and
quantum mechanics, which describes the behavior of the very
small. The goal is to create a single framework that
encompasses both realms, which has proven difficult due to
the non-intuitive nature of quantum effects and the complex
geometries involved in general relativity.
4.Question
What is the significance of quanta in the context of
quantum mechanics?
Answer:In quantum mechanics, quanta represent discrete
packets of energy that particles exchange. Unlike classical
physics, where energy transfer can occur in any amount,
quantum theory stipulates that only whole units or quanta can
be transferred, leading to unique and often counterintuitive
behaviors in atomic and subatomic particles.
5.Question
What are the potential outcomes of combining quantum
mechanics with general relativity?
Answer:The combination of quantum mechanics with
general relativity may lead to the discovery of a Unified
Theory that could explain all physical phenomena in the
universe. Remarkably, it could reveal unexpected insights
and understanding of gravity, possibly leading to the
identification of entities like gravitons — hypothetical
particles that mediate the force of gravity.
6.Question
How do the efforts of physicists like Feynman and
Bergmann contribute to our understanding of gravity?
Answer:Physicists like Feynman and Bergmann approach the
problem of gravity from different angles. While Bergmann
tries to quantize the theory of gravitation directly, Feynman
adapts the principles of quantum mechanics to apply them to
gravity, exploring the idea that gravity might work through
discrete units, promoting innovative thinking that could
ultimately yield a deeper understanding of the universe.
7.Question
What lesson can we take from the physicists' struggle
with gravity and relativity?
Answer:The ongoing struggle of physicists to unify gravity
with quantum mechanics teaches us the value of persistence
in the face of complex challenges. It underscores the idea that
where there are significant difficulties in understanding
nature, there may also lie profound discoveries waiting to be
made.
Chapter 14 | Appendix III: “The Future of Physics”
by Richard P. Feynman| Q&A
1.Question
What is Richard Feynman's perspective on predicting the
future of physics?
Answer:Feynman believes that predicting the future
of physics is challenging and cannot be done in
isolation from the political and social context of the
time. He suggests that while many people make
short-term predictions based on current trends, he
prefers to look long-term, even up to 1,000 years
into the future, to see broader possibilities.
2.Question
How does Feynman relate the future of physics to
historical events?
Answer:He compares the current flourishing of physics to
earlier 'heroic ages' in history, like that of Archimedes.
Feynman expresses concern that if a catastrophic event (like
a war) occurred, it could set back progress in physics, similar
to historical collapses that have stifled scientific
advancement.
3.Question
What does Feynman suggest about the possibility of a
'final solution' in physics?
Answer:Feynman contemplates whether a final solution to
fundamental physics laws might be unreachable, believing
that while we may find laws, questions will continue to arise,
leading to eternal exploration and discovery. He argues that
just because we've made progress doesn't mean we're at the
end.
4.Question
What alternatives does Feynman see for the development
of physics after potential societal collapse?
Answer:He posits that after a collapse, other fields might
succeed and then invigorate interest in physics once again.
Success outside of physics could foster a renewed spirit and
encourage scientific inquiry.
5.Question
What concerns does Feynman have regarding the
longevity and excitement of physics?
Answer:He expresses skepticism about whether physics can
maintain its current excitement and believes that physics may
experience slowdowns, leading to fewer discoveries and a
potential decline in interest as the questions become
increasingly complex and difficult.
6.Question
What is Feynman's ultimate view on the future of
fundamental physics?
Answer:Feynman believes that while the current age is
vibrant and full of potential, fundamental physics is not
infinite in its exploration. He anticipates that after a while,
the field may reach a plateau of discovery, although he
acknowledges the excitement of the present era.
7.Question
How does Feynman view the role of philosophy in relation
to scientific discovery?
Answer:Feynman argues that the ongoing struggle and
uncertainty in physics foster a vigorous philosophy of
science. He warns that a resolved end to fundamental
questions could lead to a stagnation in scientific philosophy.
8.Question
In what ways does Feynman think physics might evolve
or expand in the future?
Answer:He suggests that physics might merge with
disciplines like cosmology and could expand to include
explorations of historical evolution within the universe. He
sees a possibility that understanding the laws of physics may
intertwine with our comprehension of cosmic history.
9.Question
What message does Feynman impart about living in the
present age in terms of scientific discovery?
Answer:He emphasizes the uniqueness of the current age,
urging that we cherish and capitalize on the opportunities for
discovery while we can, as future ages may look back with
jealousy at the groundbreaking work being done in
fundamental physics today.

enjoys intimate discussions and small group interactions,

styles synergize when they collaborate, leading to robust
discussions and rapid advancements in theoretical physics.

What does the term 'strangeness' mean in particle physics
and how does it relate to Gell-Mann's work?
Answer:'Strangeness' is a property Gell-Mann introduced to
classify particles based on their disintegration processes. It
helps distinguish particles and contributes to categorizing
them into families, thus aiding physicists like Gell-Mann in
creating organizational charts for the myriad of particles,
much like the periodic table in chemistry.

How did Feynman’s personal experiences influence his
approach to physics?
Answer:Feynman’s approach to physics was shaped
significantly by his father's influence, who took him on
explorative walks and encouraged him to observe nature

'kick' of revelation that comes from uncovering nature's
intricacies, which he carried into his scientific pursuits,
emphasizing a hands-on, inquisitive approach.

Feynman and Gell-Mann share?
Answer:Both physicists believe in the inherent beauty and

suggesting that understanding physics is not merely
analytical, but also an encounter with something profoundly
beautiful.
6.Question
What is the significance of the discovery of the violation

Answer:The discovery that weak interactions can violate the
law of parity revolutionized nuclear physics. It challenged
long-held beliefs about symmetry in nature, paving the way
for new theories and deeper insight into radioactive decay
processes, and highlighting the need to rethink fundamental
physical laws.
7.Question

state of understanding in physics, and what does it imply?
Answer:Feynman compares the understanding of physics to
playing chess with a Martian; even though the rules and
some strategies are known, the true 'middle game' is a
mystery. This metaphor implies that while physicists have
developed substantial theories and methods of analysis, a
complete understanding of the interactions at the
fundamental level remains elusive.
8.Question
How do the personalities of Feynman and Gell-Mann
affect their reception among students and faculty?
Answer:Feynman's flamboyant and dynamic teaching style
attracts large audiences and instills excitement, making
students eager to learn. Conversely, Gell-Mann's intellectual
charm and clarity engage smaller groups in deeper
discussions, creating an intimate learning environment that
fosters critical thinking. Together, their contrasting
approaches rejuvenate the academic atmosphere around
physics.
